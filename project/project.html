Long-Term Building Load Forecasting Under Distributional Shift

AI for Climate Action - Final Report
2026, 6.S893, MIT

Buildings account for roughly 31% of U.S. greenhouse gas emissions, and electrifying heating is a primary decarbonization strategy - but it will reshape electricity demand. ISO New England projects that by 2050 the peak may shift from a ~28 GW summer peak to a winter peak of up to 57 GW, with grid upgrades on the order of tens of billions of dollars. These projections are highly uncertain: adoption rates, upgrade pathways, and weather can produce very different outcomes.

Physics-based simulation can model upgrade scenarios but needs detailed building inputs that are rarely available, while metered data is abundant but cannot say how demand will evolve under new conditions. This project bridges the two: a generative model learns scenario-induced load shifts from simulation and applies them to observed load data, producing diverse, plausible future demand scenarios for grid planning.

Framework Paired baseline and post-upgrade load trajectories are generated with physics-based simulation (NREL ResStock). A conditional generative model learns the distribution of the load change, p(ΔY | Ybase, X), conditioned on the baseline weekly load and temperature summaries. At deployment, the model takes observed current loads and samples future load scenarios directly.

Dataset The de-risking experiment uses New England households with electricity and natural gas, under the "Cold Climate Ducted Air Source Heat Pump" upgrade. Weekly windows of 168 hourly readings are summed into groups of 10-150 buildings within a weather zone to reflect real feeder diversity. Each baseline week is paired with four different post-upgrade target weeks (one-to-many), giving 405 groups and 16,200 samples, split by group, plus an 800-building holdout set.

Generative Models Both models share a backbone: a convolutional conditioning encoder (baseline curve + temperatures → c ∈ ℝ128) and an autoencoder that compresses the two-channel (electricity, gas) weekly load change into a 64-d latent. The autoencoder loss combines MAE with tail, weekly-sum, and ramp terms so it preserves the peaks, fuel-switching magnitude, and sharp transitions that matter for grid planning. In that latent space we compare a conditional normalizing flow (RealNVP, 8 affine coupling layers, exact likelihood) and a conditional diffusion model (DDPM with a denoising MLP, sampled with DDIM).

Preliminary Results Both models capture the expected heat-pump signature: higher electricity demand and lower natural gas use. The flow model gives lower ensemble-mean errors (e.g. net weekly electricity error 0.38 vs. 6.66 on the test set), while diffusion consistently produces more diverse samples and wider envelopes, especially for small 10-building groups - a trade-off between central accuracy and scenario coverage. Both degrade on unseen small groups (5 buildings) and unseen states (NY, SC), but extrapolate well to larger 200-building groups, where aggregation smooths per-building variability.

Next Steps Expand beyond a single upgrade scenario with a compositional scenario encoding (HVAC, envelope, water heating, EV, demand flexibility, adoption rate), re-simulate with projected future weather, calibrate against metered data, and evaluate how probabilistic scenarios affect peak capacity, grid investment, and retrofit policy decisions.

Bayesian Forecasting of Commercial Building Electricity Demand

Bayesian Modeling and Inference - Final Report
2026, 6.7830, MIT

Short-term commercial building load forecasting supports grid planning, peak management, and demand response. Building demand has clear temporal structure but is inherently uncertain. This project studies how modeling assumptions, inference algorithms, and specification choices affect posterior inference and forecasts, using 8 buildings from the Building Data Genome Project 2 chosen to range from stable, well-structured series to noisy series with abrupt shifts.

Two models with different inductive biases are compared. Prophet assumes a stable piecewise-linear trend with fixed Fourier seasonality (fit here with full posterior inference rather than MAP). Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) treats trend and daily/weekly seasonality as time-evolving latent states; a Kalman filter integrates over the latent trajectories, so inference only targets the variance parameters.

Step 1 - Forecast Accuracy Across Buildings and Horizons Both models are fit with ADVI and evaluated with 7-fold rolling-origin validation at 6-hour to 5-day horizons. Prophet stays stable across horizons (median MAPE ~10%) with a nearly flat prediction interval, but varies widely between buildings. BSTS is better at short horizons (~6% median MAPE at 6 hours) but degrades as the horizon grows, and its 90% prediction interval widens quickly as uncertainty builds up in the drifting latent states. Regular buildings such as Hog_office are easy for both models; on irregular buildings, BSTS has a large short-horizon advantage that narrows over longer horizons.

Step 2 - Inference: ADVI vs. NUTS Good forecasts don't guarantee a reliable posterior, so ADVI (including a longer run) and NUTS are compared on a regular and an irregular building. For Prophet, ADVI and NUTS agree, so performance is limited by model structure rather than inference. BSTS can fit the training curve almost perfectly, but the methods find noticeably different variance posteriors that explain the past equally well while implying different futures. This is weak identifiability: a spike can be read as noise, seasonal drift, or trend movement. Running more NUTS chains did not help and sometimes made forecasts worse.

Step 3 - Decomposition as an Output The posterior decomposition is a result in its own right. Prophet extracts a clean, interpretable structure (a nearly flat trend with repeating weekly and daily patterns) even when it can't explain the irregularities. BSTS adapts more, but ADVI and NUTS split local variation across components differently, which shows how much of the load behavior remains unexplained. The models don't just predict the future; they impose different explanations of the past.

Takeaway Bayesian load forecasting is a problem of trade-offs. Prophet is stable and interpretable when patterns are regular; BSTS adapts better but is sensitive to uncertainty accumulation and weak identifiability. More flexibility or more exhaustive inference does not necessarily improve the forecast. The right choice depends on whether stability, adaptivity, uncertainty, or interpretability matters most.

PointPROP

GitHub Link
2025 Fall, 6.7960, MIT
Instructor: Sara Beery, Kaiming He, Omar Khattab
Collaborator: Eric Kyle Cheung

PointPROP is a deep learning–based surrogate model for point-in-time solar ray occlusion directly on urban LiDAR point clouds. Given:
  • a context point cloud pts_context ∈ ℝN×3,
  • a set of test points pts_test ∈ ℝM×3, and
  • a solar ray direction ray ∈ ℝ3,
the model predicts whether each test point is occluded or visible along that ray. Formally, we learn a binary classifier:
f(pts_context, pts_test, ray) ∈ {0,1}M

Step 1 - Baseline Comparison (Transformer vs. MLP, ± Fourier Features) We compare transformer-based and MLP-based architectures, each with and without Fourier positional embeddings, to evaluate their inductive biases for occlusion prediction. Fourier features improve convergence and slightly lower training loss, but introduce higher validation variance. Overall, all four models achieve nearly identical test performance, indicating that architectural choice and positional encoding have limited impact on final classification trade-offs. The MLP with Fourier features achieves the lowest loss and most stable convergence, while qualitative results show that models capture global occlusion patterns well but struggle with finer geometric details.

Step 2 - Capacity and Resolution Analysis We investigate whether model performance is limited by aggressive point cloud downsampling rather than architectural choice. By comparing 8× and 4× downsampling in the encode, we test the impact of spatial resolution and model capacity. Results show that reducing downsampling (4×) improves stability for the MLP without sacrificing accuracy, while the transformer becomes more prone to overfitting when given denser inputs. Importantly, lowering feature dimensions does not harm performance but significantly reduces parameter count and inference time. Overall, a shallower 4× downsampling MLP offers the best trade-off between accuracy, stability, and efficiency, suggesting that resolution is the primary bottleneck.

Step 3 - Incorporating Local Geometric Bias To address the model’s difficulty in capturing fine-grained geometric patterns, we introduce a local pooling branch inspired by PointNet++. In addition to global pooling, each test point aggregates features from its K nearest context neighbors using relative positional encoding and shared MLPs, producing a local descriptor that is combined with global and point-wise features for prediction. This local geometric bias significantly improves performance with minimal increase in parameters and no additional inference cost. Qualitative results show sharper probability maps and better recovery of small occluded regions, confirming that explicitly modeling local structure substantially enhances fine-detail prediction.

Bluebike Rebalancing

Multi-stage mixed-integer bike system optimization - Final Report
2025 Fall, 6.C57J, MIT
Instructor: Alexandre Jacquillat, Saurabh Amin
Collaborator: Ray Law, Kara Chou

This project develops a predictive rebalancing strategy for the Bluebikes system in Greater Boston, which supports over 27 million trips across 600 stations and 5,500 bikes. Due to highly stochastic and spatially uneven demand, stations frequently become either empty or full, leading to user dissatisfaction and lost ridership. To address this, we formulate a two-stage optimization framework that determines optimal initial bike allocations and truck routing plans before daily operations begin. By leveraging historical demand data, the model minimizes unmet demand and rebalancing costs, providing a data-driven baseline schedule that improves system reliability while reducing operational inefficiencies.

Subproblem 1 determines the optimal initial distribution of bikes across stations at the start of the day. The goal is to reduce user dissatisfaction—caused by stations becoming nearly empty or nearly full—while keeping bike flows consistent with expected demand patterns. The model allocates a fixed total number of bikes across stations, ensures that bike counts always stay within station capacity, and penalizes large deviations from forecasted net flows.

Subproblem 2 determines how to physically rebalance the system to achieve the target bike distribution computed in Subproblem 1. We model the network as a routing problem in which a limited fleet of trucks picks up and drops off bikes across stations. The optimization jointly decides truck routes and loading actions to move the system as close as possible to the desired inventory levels, while minimizing travel distance, handling costs, and unmet targets.

Scan-to-Assessment

Scan-to-simulation for property assessment

×

Scan-to-Assessment

AEC Tech Hack 2024, NYC - GitHub Link
Team Project

This project develops a tool for real estate value assessment through indoor comfort analysis, including daylight, visual, and spatial factors. It preprocesses raw point clouds into rationalized geometry using machine learning and computer vision, enabling simulations with Ladybug in Grasshopper. Results are integrated into a web viewer for easy interaction, empowering residents and real estate owners to assess and improve property value. My role was leading the implementation of the geometry rationalization process.

The 3D point cloud is segmented into building elements (e.g., floor, ceiling, walls, windows). Floor points are projected onto a 2D plane and padded, which is processed with edge detection to extract the building footprint. The footprint is then simplified and refined for clarity. The Z-min and Z-max values of walls are calculated to determine the room height. For windows, the two furthest endpoints on the footprint are identified to define horizontal extents, and their Z-min and Z-max values are calculated. for the parametric reconstruction of windows.

Water Chandelier

Tallinn Architecture Biennale, 2022

×

Friendship WC

Summer 2022, Cornell University, Ecological Action Lab
Design Leader: Freddo Daneshvaran
Team Member: Amber Su, Ann Ren, Kate Heath
Design Advisor: Caroline O’Donnell, Iris Xiaoxue Ma, Martin Miller
Photo by Tõnu Tunnel

By 2030, almost half the world will be living with high water stress. In some areas, the entire daily ration of water is equivalent to a single flush of potable water in North American toilets. Friendship WC connects the materiality of plastic and water. The chandelier is continuously releasing and refilled with water; the amount is equal to one person’s daily flushed-away potable water. Throughout this project, I had been working on the digital fabrication using 3D print, CNC, and metal casting; and parametric design process.
